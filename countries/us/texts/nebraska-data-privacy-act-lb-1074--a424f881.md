Summary

The Nebraska Data Privacy Act (NDPA), signed on April 17, 2024, has applied since January 1, 2025. It covers most businesses that are not small businesses, sets no revenue or consumer-count threshold, and keeps a permanent 30-day cure period. This overview explains who must comply, consumer rights, controller duties, and enforcement.

## At a Glance

- The NDPA has applied since January 1, 2025, and only the Nebraska Attorney General can enforce it. There is no private right of action.
- It has no revenue or consumer-count threshold. It reaches businesses that operate in Nebraska or serve its residents, process or sell personal data, and are not small businesses under federal law.
- Small businesses fall outside most duties but may not sell sensitive data without the consumer’s prior consent.
- Most processing runs on an opt-out model. Consent is required for sensitive data, including data from known children under 13.
- Controllers must answer consumer requests within 45 days, publish a privacy notice, and document data protection assessments for higher-risk processing.
- The 30-day cure period has no expiration date. Civil penalties can reach USD 7,500 per violation.

Governor Jim Pillen signed the Nebraska Data Privacy Act (NDPA) into law on April 17, 2024, making Nebraska the sixteenth state to enact a comprehensive consumer privacy law. It has applied since January 1, 2025, a shorter lead time than many U.S. state privacy laws had.

This overview explains who must comply with the NDPA, what rights it gives Nebraska residents, what controllers must do, and how the Attorney General enforces the law.

## What Is the Nebraska Data Privacy Act?

The Nebraska Data Privacy Act (NDPA), enacted through Legislative Bill 1074 and codified at Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 87-1101 to 87-1130, gives Nebraska residents rights over their personal data. It places duties on businesses that operate in Nebraska or produce products or services that Nebraska residents consume.

The NDPA defines a consumer as an individual who is a resident of Nebraska and is “acting only in an individual or household context.” The definition excludes Nebraska residents acting in a commercial or employment context.

Like most other U.S. states, Nebraska follows an opt-out model for most processing, with opt-in consent required for sensitive data. The NDPA requires businesses to explain clearly:

- What personal data they collect
- Purpose for collecting personal data
- Third parties with whom they share personal data
- How consumers can opt out of the collection and processing of their personal data for certain purposes

## Who Must Comply With the Nebraska Data Privacy Act?

The NDPA applies to a person or business that meets all three of the following conditions:

- Conducts business in Nebraska or produces a product or service consumed by Nebraska residents
- Processes or sells personal data
- Is not a small business as determined under the federal Small Business Act

The NDPA relies on the federal Small Business Act, as it existed on January 1, 2024, to define a small business. The U.S. Small Business Administration sets size standards by industry, using either employee count or average annual revenue, so no single cutoff fits every company. A business does not need to be located in Nebraska for the law to apply.

Small businesses are not wholly outside the law. Section 18 bars them from selling sensitive data without the consumer’s prior consent, and a violation can bring civil penalties.

Like the Texas Data Privacy and Security Act (TDPSA), the NDPA does not contain a threshold for annual revenue, revenue from the sale of personal data, or the number of residents whose personal data is processed or sold. Many other U.S. state-level data privacy laws, including the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) and Virginia Consumer Data Protection Act (VCDPA), contain these thresholds that businesses must meet for the law to apply.

### Exemptions to NDPA Compliance

Certain entities are exempt from complying with the NDPA, including:

- State agencies or political subdivisions
- Financial institutions or affiliates subject to the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA)
- Covered entities or business associates governed by the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act
- Nonprofit organizations
- Higher education institutions
- Electric and natural gas public utilities

Data that is exempt from the law includes:

- Protected healthcare-related information
- Research data
- Data processed in an employment, job-applicant, or contractor context, including emergency-contact and benefits data
- Data created for or collected under several federal laws, including, among others:
  - HIPAA
  - Health Care Quality Improvement Act
  - Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA)
  - Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act
  - Farm Credit Act (FCA)
  - Driver’s Privacy Protection Act
  - Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA)

## Definitions Under the NDPA

The NDPA defines the key terms that determine what data it protects and which processing activities it covers.

### Personal Data Under the NDPA

The Nebraska privacy law defines personal data as “any information, including sensitive data, that is linked or reasonably linkable to an identified or identifiable individual, and includes pseudonymous data when the data is used by a controller or processor in conjunction with additional information that reasonably links the data to an identified or identifiable individual.” The definition excludes de-identified data or publicly available information.

The NDPA, unlike some other state-level consumer data privacy laws, does not provide specific examples of personal data. Common types that businesses collect include:

- Name
- Email address
- Phone number
- Social Security number
- Driver’s license number

Online identifiers, such as cookie IDs or device identifiers, can also count when they are linked or reasonably linkable to an individual.

### Sensitive Data Under the NDPA

Sensitive data is a category of personal data that receives stronger protection under the NDPA. It includes:

- Racial or ethnic origin
- Religious beliefs
- Mental or physical health diagnosis
- Sexual orientation
- Citizenship or immigration status
- Genetic or biometric data processed for the purpose of uniquely identifying an individual
- Personal data collected from a known child, meaning a child under 13 whose age the controller actually knows or willfully disregards
- Precise geolocation data that can accurately identify an individual’s specific location within a radius of 1,750 feet or 533.4 meters

### Consent Under the NDPA

The law defines consent as “a clear and affirmative act signifying a consumer’s freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous agreement to process personal data relating to the consumer, including a statement written by electronic means or any other unambiguous affirmative action by the consumer.”

The definition specifically excludes:

- Acceptance of a general or broad terms of use or similar document that contains descriptions of personal data processing along with other, unrelated information
- Hovering over, muting, pausing, or closing a given piece of content
- Agreement obtained through the use of dark patterns

### Controller Under the NDPA

The law defines a controller as “an individual or other person that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purpose and means of processing personal data.“

A controller is also known as a “data controller” under some laws and must comply with the responsibilities of controllers under the law to protect personal data.

### Processor Under the NDPA

A processor under the law is “a person that processes personal data on behalf of a controller.”

“Person” could mean an individual or natural person, a company, or other organization and is bound by the obligations the NDPA places on processors.

### Sale of Personal Data Under the NDPA

The Nebraska data privacy law defines sale of personal data as “the exchange of personal data for monetary or other valuable consideration by the controller to a third party.“

Sale does not include disclosure of personal data:

- To a processor that processes the personal data on the controller’s behalf
- To a third party for the purposes of providing a product or service the consumer has requested
- To the controller’s affiliate, including transfer of personal data
- That the consumer intentionally made available to the public through a mass media channel not restricted to a specific audience
- To a third party as an asset that is part of a proposed or actual merger, acquisition, bankruptcy, or other transaction including transfer of personal data

### Targeted Advertising Under the NDPA

The NDPA defines targeted advertising as “displaying to a consumer an advertisement that is selected based on personal data obtained from that consumer’s activities over time and across nonaffiliated websites or online applications to predict the consumer’s preferences or interests.”

The definition excludes:

- Ads based on activities within a controller’s own websites or online apps
- Ads based on the context of a consumer’s current search query, visit to a website, or online app
- Ads directed to a consumer in response to the consumer’s request for information or feedback
- Processing of personal data solely for measuring or reporting ad performance, reach, or frequency

## Consumer Rights Under the NDPA

The NDPA gives consumers several rights to protect their personal data and control how it’s used. Decisions with legal or similarly significant effects are those that result in the provision or denial of financial and lending services, housing, insurance, health care, education enrollment, employment, criminal justice, or access to basic necessities such as food and water.

A parent or legal guardian can exercise these rights on behalf of a known child. The NDPA gives consumers no private right of action, so they cannot sue a controller directly. They can submit a complaint to the Attorney General.

#### NDPA Consumer Rights

##### Right to access

Consumers can confirm whether or not the controller is processing their personal data and can access their data, with some exceptions.

##### Right to correction

Consumers have the right to have any inaccuracies in their personal data that the controller holds corrected, taking into account the nature of the personal data and purposes of processing.

##### Right to deletion

Consumers can request the deletion of any personal data provided by, or obtained about, them, with exceptions.

##### Right to data portability

Consumers can obtain a copy of their personal data that they previously provided to the controller, in a ready usable format, with some exceptions.

##### Right to opt out

Consumers can opt out of the processing of their personal data for targeted advertising, for the sale of personal data, and for profiling that furthers a decision with legal or similarly significant effects.

## Controller Obligations Under the NDPA

The NDPA places a defined set of duties on controllers, the businesses that decide why and how personal data is processed.

### Consumer Rights Requests Under the NDPA

Controllers must inform consumers about:

- Their rights under the law
- How they can exercise these rights
- Contact details for the controller
- How to appeal a decision if a consumer request is rejected

This information is typically included in a privacy notice or policy, which controllers must publish as required by the NDPA.

Controllers must offer two or more secure and reliable methods for submitting requests, chosen with regard to how consumers normally interact with the business.

If the controller has a website, it must include a mechanism for consumers to submit requests for information. Controllers operating exclusively online with a direct consumer relationship only need to provide an email address for consumer request submissions.

Controllers have 45 days to respond to consumer requests, with a possible extension of another 45 days if reasonably necessary to comply. If an extension is needed, the controller must inform the consumer before the initial 45-day period ends. If the controller cannot reasonably verify the consumer’s identity, additional verification requests can be made or the request can be declined. In case of a decline, the controller must notify the consumer within 45 days from the receipt of the request, and must inform the consumer of the reason for denial and the appeal process.

Controllers must respond free of charge up to twice a year per consumer. They may charge a reasonable fee for, or decline, a request that is manifestly unfounded, excessive, or repetitive, and the controller bears the burden of showing that.

Controllers have 60 days to respond to appeals. The appeal process must be conspicuously available and similar to the process for making a request. If a controller denies an appeal, it must give the consumer an online mechanism to submit a complaint to the Attorney General.

### Purpose Limitation and Data Minimization Under the NDPA

Controllers must limit the personal data they collect to what is adequate, relevant, and reasonably necessary for the purposes they have disclosed to the consumer. Processing for a purpose that is neither reasonably necessary to nor compatible with those disclosed purposes requires the consumer’s consent. These rules support transparency and keep collection tied to the stated purposes.

### Data Security Under the NDPA

The Nebraska privacy law requires controllers to protect the confidentiality, integrity, and accessibility of personal data. For this purpose, they must establish, implement, and maintain reasonable administrative, technical, and physical security measures that are suitable for the volume and nature of the personal data being processed.

### Data Protection Assessments Under the NDPA

Controllers must conduct and document a data protection assessment for each of the following processing activities:

- Targeted advertising
- The sale of personal data
- Profiling that presents a reasonably foreseeable risk of:
  - Unfair or deceptive treatment of, or unlawful disparate impact on, any consumer
  - Financial, physical, or reputational injury
  - An intrusion on a consumer’s solitude, seclusion, or private affairs that would be offensive to a reasonable person
  - Other substantial injury to any consumer
- Processing of sensitive data
- Any processing that presents a heightened risk of harm to a consumer

The Attorney General can request a relevant assessment through a civil investigative demand. Assessments are confidential and exempt from public-records disclosure. One assessment can cover a comparable set of processing operations, and an assessment prepared for another law can count if its scope and effect are reasonably comparable.

### Consent Requirements Under the NDPA

Nebraska follows an opt-out model for most personal data processing, so businesses can generally collect and process data without first asking for consent. A controller must, however, obtain the consumer’s consent before processing sensitive data, and before processing personal data for a purpose that is neither reasonably necessary to nor compatible with the purposes it disclosed.

For the sale of personal data and targeted advertising, the NDPA relies on opt-out rights. A controller that does either must clearly and conspicuously disclose it and explain how consumers can opt out.

For children’s data, the NDPA points to the federal Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA). Personal data collected from a known child is sensitive data and must be processed in line with COPPA. A controller that meets COPPA’s verifiable parental consent requirements is treated as meeting the NDPA’s parental consent requirements for data collected online.

### Nondiscrimination Under the NDPA

The NDPA prohibits controllers from discriminating against consumers who exercise their rights under the law. This includes denying them goods or services, charging different prices, or offering different quality levels to those consumers. For example, a business cannot deny a good or service, charge a different price, or offer a lower quality of service because a consumer exercised an NDPA right.

The rule does not require a controller to provide a product or service that depends on personal data it does not collect or maintain. It also does not prohibit different prices or service levels tied to a consumer’s voluntary participation in a bona fide loyalty, rewards, premium feature, discount, or club card program.

Additionally, controllers must comply with state and federal discrimination laws and cannot process personal information in violation of these laws.

### Privacy Notice Under the NDPA

The Nebraska privacy law requires controllers to publish a clear, accessible, and meaningful privacy notice, which must include information on:

- categories of personal data processed, including sensitive personal data, if any
- purposes for processing personal data
- how consumers may exercise their rights under the law, as well as how they may appeal the controller’s decision regarding a request
- categories of personal data shared with third parties, if any
- categories of third parties who receive personal data, if any
- a description of each method through which a consumer may submit a request to exercise their rights

The privacy notice or privacy policy must be clearly accessible to consumers and is usually published on the controller’s website, with a link in the footer that makes it easy to find and accessible from all pages.

### Data Processing Agreements Under the NDPA

Controllers must enter into contracts with processors that govern data processing procedures. While the law does not specifically use the term “data processing agreement,” this contract serves the same function as data processing agreements in other data privacy laws, such as the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and Virginia’s privacy law.

The contract or data processing agreement must clearly outline:

- Clear instructions for processing
- The nature and purpose of processing
- The type of data subject to processing
- The duration of processing
- The rights and obligations of both parties
- A requirement that each person processing the data is subject to a duty of confidentiality
- A requirement that the processor delete or return personal data at the controller’s direction once the service ends, unless the law requires retention
- A requirement that the processor make available the information needed to show its compliance and allow reasonable assessments by the controller, or arrange an independent assessment
- A requirement that any subcontractor be engaged under a written contract imposing the same obligations

Processors must also assist controllers in meeting their duties related to the Nebraska data privacy law, including responding to consumer rights requests and security of personal data processing.

The NDPA does not relieve a controller or processor of the liabilities that come with its role in the processing relationship. It does include a safe harbor of a kind found in other state privacy laws. A controller or processor that discloses personal data to a third party in compliance with the law does not violate it if the recipient later breaks the law, unless the discloser had actual knowledge, at the time of disclosure, that the recipient intended to violate it. Likewise, a recipient that lawfully receives personal data is not liable for the earlier violations of the party that disclosed it.

### Universal Opt-Out Mechanisms Under the NDPA

The NDPA lets a consumer designate an authorized agent to opt out of targeted advertising and the sale of personal data. The consumer can do this through technology such as a browser setting or extension, or a global device setting. Global Privacy Control (GPC) is the best-known example. The statute does not name GPC, and the mechanism does not extend to profiling opt-outs.

A controller must honor an opt-out request received this way if it can verify, with commercially reasonable effort, the consumer’s identity and the agent’s authority. It need not comply if the request is not clear and unambiguous, if it cannot verify that the consumer is a Nebraska resident, if it cannot process the request, or if it does not process similar requests to comply with other states’ laws. Businesses that already honor GPC elsewhere should expect to honor it for Nebraska visitors.

The technology itself must not rely on a default setting. It must require the consumer to make an affirmative, freely given, and unambiguous choice.

## Enforcement of the NDPA

The Nebraska Attorney General has exclusive enforcement authority under the NDPA. Consumers do not have a private right of action but can report potential violations or denials of their privacy rights to the Attorney General’s office. Before bringing an enforcement action, the Attorney General must provide the implicated party with written notice of the alleged violations.

Where the Attorney General has reasonable cause to believe a controller or processor has violated the law, it can issue a civil investigative demand for documents, including relevant data protection assessments.

### Cure Period Under the NDPA

The Attorney General must give written notice at least 30 days before bringing an action, and may not proceed if the organization cures the identified violation within that window. To qualify, the organization must give the Attorney General a written statement that the violation was cured, supporting documentation showing how it was cured, and an express written statement that it will not commit the violation again. The NDPA has no sunset date for its cure period.

### Fines and Penalties Under the NDPA

A controller or processor that violates the NDPA after the cure period, or that breaches the written statement it gave the Attorney General, can be sued by the Attorney General in the name of the State. The Attorney General can seek an injunction, civil penalties of up to USD 7,500 per violation, or both, and can recover reasonable attorney’s fees and other expenses of investigating and bringing the action.

As of November 2026, the Attorney General has not publicly announced an action brought under the NDPA itself. The office has pursued data privacy cases under other laws, however. In July 2025, it sued General Motors and OnStar under the Nebraska Consumer Protection Act and the Uniform Deceptive Trade Practices Act over the alleged collection and sale of drivers’ data without their knowledge or consent.

## Consent Management and the NDPA

The NDPA follows the opt-out model common to U.S. state privacy laws. Businesses can generally collect and process personal data without prior consent, but sensitive data, including children’s data, requires it.

Consumers must be able to opt out of the sale of personal data, targeted advertising, and certain profiling, and businesses must present that option clearly, usually in the privacy policy or privacy notice. Many websites also use a banner with a clear link or button for opting out. A consent management platform (CMP) such as Usercentrics Web CMP or App CMP can help by managing consent for cookies and tracking technologies, honoring opt-out signals such as GPC, and holding non-essential tools back where a visitor’s consent is required. It also documents the categories of data collected, the purposes, and the third parties involved.

With no unified federal privacy law in the U.S., businesses may need to follow several state and international laws at once, depending on where their visitors are. A CMP can show each visitor the banner that fits their location, which supports compliance with state laws such as the NDPA and international regulations such as the GDPR.

## Preparing for NDPA Compliance

The NDPA has applied since January 1, 2025, so the task now is to confirm that your practices meet its requirements. Businesses that already follow the Texas law, which the NDPA closely resembles, should find much of the groundwork in place. A privacy by design approach also benefits the whole organization, not only regulatory work.

A practical sequence for most businesses:

- Confirm whether the NDPA applies to you, including whether the small business rule or an exemption covers you.
- Map the personal data you process, and flag any sensitive data that needs consent.
- Update your privacy notice, offer two or more ways to submit requests, and set up a working appeals process.
- Offer opt-outs for targeted advertising and sales, and honor browser-based signals such as GPC where the law requires.
- Document data protection assessments, and review your processor contracts.

A CMP can help manage the cookies and tracking technologies in use on your websites. Privacy law keeps changing with technology and consumer expectations. A qualified legal professional or data privacy expert, such as a Data Protection Officer, can help you interpret the NDPA for your circumstances and track new developments.
